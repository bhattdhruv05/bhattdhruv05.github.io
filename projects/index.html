01 / DFI 4.0

Smart Gripper Design and Robot Cell Simulation

TU Clausthal · Group project

Developed a motor-driven, three-jaw scroll gripper concept for cylindrical parts, taking it from concept evaluation and CAD assembly through virtual commissioning and process analytics. The scroll and radial guides synchronize the jaws to centre the workpiece. In Siemens NX MCD, built an ABB GoFa pick-and-place cell with part detection, conveyor stop logic and condition-based gripping, transfer and placement. Developed models using a supplied 12,212-cycle dataset to analyse energy use, cycle time and faults, and compared their performance under random and chronological validation. The project brought mechanism design, automation logic and data analysis together in one engineering workflow.

Mechanism designVirtual commissioningData analytics

What I learned

This project taught me to consider the gripper as part of a complete automation system. The jaw mechanism, robot motion, sensors and sequence logic all had to work together for the simulated cell to handle parts consistently. Analysing cycle data then showed me how energy use, timing and faults could inform decisions about that same process. I gained a clearer understanding of how mechanical design, automation and data influence one another.

02 / IFS

Robotic Bending with FEM Springback Correction

TU Clausthal · Engineering project

Built a Python-based tool to plan robotic sheet-metal bending with springback correction. The tool runs LS-DYNA simulations remotely, extracts the deformed sheet-edge geometry and measures its deviation from the target position. Models trained on simulation results predict a Z-axis correction, which is then checked in a new FEM run and can be refined further. A graphical interface brings case selection, simulation, correction and result review into one workflow.

PythonFEMRobotic bending

What I learned

I learned why a commanded bend position cannot be treated as the final sheet geometry after unloading. Automating simulations and extracting measurements from FEM results taught me how to turn detailed model output into useful engineering data. Checking a predicted correction with another simulation showed me the value of combining data-driven estimates with a physics-based verification step.

03 / ELMEX

Automated Visual Inspection of Terminal Links

Elmex Controls · Industrial project

Implemented a multi-camera inspection process for electrical terminal links in an assembly environment. I configured reference images and feature-specific checks across different views to identify issues such as missing or misoriented components, bent current bars and molding defects. Setting acceptance ranges and reviewing repeated inspection runs helped distinguish actual defects from normal variation between parts. The configured system classified links as OK or NOT OK for separation on the line.

Terminal-link inspectionMachine visionQuality checks

What I learned

I learned that detecting a feature is only part of a useful inspection system; it must also tolerate acceptable variation without rejecting good parts. Configuring the camera views and inspection ranges showed me how alignment, lighting and threshold choices affect the result. Reviewing inspection runs against the actual parts taught me to refine the checks using production evidence.

04 / ELMEX

Automated Spring Insertion Prototype

Elmex Controls · B.Tech project

Designed and refined mechanisms to automate spring insertion in terminal links, progressing from a punch-and-die approach to a guided roller-press design. The final prototype concept combines a spring feeder, pneumatic movement and guide rails to present the spring and position the terminal link. The link then travels beneath a roller that presses the spring into place. Developing the sequence as connected mechanisms made spring orientation, part alignment and controlled movement central to the design.

Prototype designPneumaticsSpring insertion

What I learned

I learned to break a short manual assembly task into separate actions: feed the spring, align it with the terminal link, move the part and seat the spring. Comparing punch-and-die and roller-press concepts showed me how guidance and clearance influence the mechanism, not just the pressing force. Developing the automatic sequence taught me to consider how reliably one step hands the part to the next.

05 / CAM

CAM Process Planning for a Gearbox Housing

TU Clausthal · Two-person practical

Developed and simulated milling toolpaths for a gearbox housing component on a three-axis vertical milling machine. I planned four workpiece orientations to access different faces of the part, using operations including adaptive milling and floor facing. The CAM plan brought the setups and machining operations together into a practical process sequence.

CAMFixturingManufacturing

What I learned

Planning four orientations showed me how strongly three-axis machining depends on part access and setup decisions. I learned to consider which faces could be reached in each orientation before choosing and ordering the milling operations. Simulating adaptive milling and floor facing helped me connect individual toolpaths to the overall machining sequence.

06 / RESEARCH

Copper Alloy Notch Support Modelling

TU Clausthal · Interdisciplinary Research Project

Developed an FKM-compatible model to estimate how much notch support copper alloys provide under cyclic loading. We evaluated fatigue-test results from smooth and notched specimens using the DIN 50100 staircase method, calculated fatigue notch factors and checked the stress-concentration estimates with finite-element analysis. The model relates notch support to material tensile strength and the stress gradient at the notch. We then turned the calibrated relationship into a design chart and spreadsheet that estimate the support factor from the alloy strength and component geometry.

FatigueModel fittingCopper alloys

What I learned

I learned why the highest elastic stress at a notch does not, by itself, describe a component’s fatigue strength. Working through the test data and finite-element checks showed me how experimental and numerical results must connect before a material model can be calibrated. I also learned how to turn a fitted relationship into a practical design chart while keeping its use within the range supported by the data.

07 / IEP

Real-Time Fatigue Damage Assessment

TU Clausthal · Four-person team

Developed an Arduino-based device to assess fatigue damage as strain data arrives, allowing the analysis to run during a test rather than after data collection. The processing sequence filters small signal fluctuations, identifies turning points and applies rainflow counting to extract load cycles. Because the device has limited memory, the cycle-counting method uses a bounded stack instead of storing the entire signal. The counted cycles are used with an S–N relationship and Miner’s rule to estimate accumulated damage. The device saves cycle data and a concise status report to an SD card, and we compared its cycle counts with a Python reference to check the results.

Embedded systemsSensingRainflow counting

What I learned

I learned how to turn fatigue theory into an online process that updates as new data arrives. Implementing the filter and rainflow counting on a device with limited memory showed me that signal handling and efficient storage matter as much as the damage calculation. Comparing the cycle counts with a Python reference taught me to verify the processing steps before trusting the final damage estimate.
